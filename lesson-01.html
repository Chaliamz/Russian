Ру́сский язы́к · Russian · Lesson One

Уро́к Пе́рвый

“Lesson One” — oo-ROK PYER-vy

By the end of this page you can introduce yourself, say where you're from, say what you do, ask all three back, and understand the reply. Not recognise it — produce it, cold, without assembling the sentence in English first.

01 — ФУНДА́МЕНТ / THE FOUNDATION

The 33 letters, and the six that lie to you

You already decode Cyrillic — slowly. Slow decoding is not a knowledge problem, it's a reflex problem, and reflexes are built by volume at speed, not by studying the table again. Read this table once, then never study it again; drill it instead.

Six letters are actively hostile: they look like Latin letters you know and make completely different sounds. Those are marked in red below. Every millisecond you lose to Russian reading is lost on those six.

The trap

Your eye reads Р as P, Н as H, С as C, В as B, У as Y, Х as X. They are r · n · s · v · oo · kh. The word РЕСТОРА́Н (ree-sta-RAN) looks like “PECTOPAH” and means restaurant. Until that stops happening, everything else is slower than it should be.

02 — ГЛА́ВНОЕ ПРА́ВИЛО / THE MAIN RULE

Russian has no word for “is”

In the present tense, Russian simply deletes the verb to be. There is no am, no is, no are. You put the two things next to each other and the sentence is finished.

Why this matters more than it looks

This is the first place where translating from English breaks down. If you find yourself hunting for the verb, you are still constructing an English sentence and converting it. Russians don't delete the verb — for them it was never there. Train the habit now, in week one, and you skip a mistake that plagues learners for years.

But it comes back in the past and the future — был / бу́ду

The verb only disappears in the present. Past and future need it, and the past tense agrees with the speaker's gender — this is a permanent feature of Russian, not a beginner detail.

03 — МЕСТОИМЕ́НИЯ / PRONOUNS

Eight pronouns, and why вы is a social decision

ты or вы — get this wrong and it's felt, not just heard

Using ты (ty) with an older stranger, an official, or a shop assistant reads as disrespectful or aggressive. Using вы (vy) with a close friend reads as cold, or sarcastic. There is no neutral option — you choose every time you open your mouth.

04 — КАК ТЕБЯ́ ЗОВУ́Т? / YOUR NAME

“My name is” is really “they call me”

There is no verb “to be called” used the way English uses it. Russians say they call me Nikos — with an invisible, unnamed “they”. The word меня́ is not “I”, it is the object form: me.

The whole family of it — меня́ / тебя́ / его́ / её …

Change only the first word and you can name anybody in the room. The verb зову́т (za-VOOT) never changes.

Sound trap

его́ is not "ye-GO". The letter г in these endings is pronounced v: ye-VO. Same in ничего́ (nee-chee-VO) — nothing, and сего́дня (see-VOD-nya) — today.

05 — ОТКУ́ДА ТЫ? / WHERE YOU'RE FROM

из + роди́тельный паде́ж — your first case

The preposition из (eez) — from, out of — forces the country name to change its ending. That changed form is the genitive case. You do not need the theory yet; you need the pattern, which is almost entirely regular.

The pattern — –ия becomes –ии

The rule in one line

Country ends in –ия → change to –ии. Ends in –а → change to –ы (or –и after к, г, х, ж, ш, ч, щ). Ends in a consonant → add –а. That covers roughly nine countries out of ten.

Islands take с, not из

You come off an island, not out of it. с Ки́пра (s KEEP-ra) — from Cyprus. с Кри́та (s KREE-ta) — from Crete. Same logic sends you на Кипр (na KEEPR) — to Cyprus, never в Кипр.

06 — ГДЕ ТЫ ЖИВЁШЬ? / WHERE YOU LIVE

в + предло́жный паде́ж — location

Second case, second pattern. в (v) — in — plus a city forces the prepositional case, which for most nouns is simply –е on the end. This is the easiest case in the language and it appears constantly.

One preposition, two cases, two meanings

Я живу́ в Москве́ (v mask-VYE) — I live in Moscow. Location.
Я е́ду в Москву́ (v mask-VOO) — I'm going to Moscow. Direction.
Same preposition. One letter of difference. Completely different sentence. This is what cases do — they carry the meaning that English carries with word order and extra prepositions.

07 — ЧЕМ ТЫ ЗАНИМА́ЕШЬСЯ? / WHAT YOU DO

Your job, in the твори́тельный паде́ж

Two ways to ask. The casual one is broader — “what are you occupied with” — and is what you'll actually hear at a party. The direct one asks specifically for a job title and forces the instrumental case, because in Russian you work as something, using it like a tool.

Job titles — я рабо́таю + –ом / –ем

The escape hatch

If your job has no clean Russian word — and “works with data in markets” does not — do what Russians do and describe the activity instead: Я рабо́таю с да́нными (s DAN-ny-mee) — I work with data. Or Я рабо́таю на себя́ (na see-BYA) — I work for myself. Nobody will push further on a first meeting.

08 — ПРИВЕ́ТСТВИЯ / GREETINGS

Hello has a clock attached to it

Russian greetings are time-bound and register-bound at the same time. Choosing wrong isn't a grammar error — it's a social one, and it's noticed immediately.

Спокойной ночи is not “good night” as a greeting

Споко́йной но́чи (spa-KOY-nay NO-chee) means sleep well and is said only when someone is going to bed. If you meet someone at 11pm, the greeting is still до́брый ве́чер (DOB-ry VYE-chir) — good evening.

09 — ВЫЖИВА́НИЕ / THE SURVIVAL KIT

Eleven phrases that keep a conversation alive

These matter more than any vocabulary list. A conversation dies the moment you don't understand and can't say so. These phrases buy you repetition, slower speech, and translation — which means the conversation continues, and every extra minute of it is training.

10 — ПРОИЗНОШЕ́НИЕ / PRONUNCIATION LAB

Eight sound rules that make you understandable

Russian spelling and Russian speech are two different systems. These eight rules bridge them. Fix these and you will be understood with broken grammar; ignore them and perfect grammar won't save you.

Minimal pairs — say both, hear the difference

If these sound identical to you right now, that's normal and it's fixable. Play each pair five times before you try to produce them.

11 — ЧИ́СЛА / NUMBERS

Zero to a hundred, and your age

You need numbers on day one — prices, times, your age, your phone number. Learn the shape of these now; the grammar that follows them (and it is genuinely strange) can wait for lesson four.

Saying your age — мне … лет

Russian doesn't say “I am thirty”. It says to me — thirty years. You are in the dative case, and the years belong to you rather than the other way round.

12 — ДИАЛО́Г / PUTTING IT TOGETHER

Everything above, in eight lines

This is the target. Read it, then play the whole thing and speak over the top of it — same speed, same rhythm, same intonation. That's called shadowing and it is the single highest-yield thing you can do with fifteen minutes.

13 — ПРОВЕ́РКА / THE TEST

Answer first. Then open it.

Reading an answer teaches you nothing; failing to retrieve one and then reading it teaches you a great deal. Say each answer out loud before you click. Getting it wrong is the point of the exercise.

14 — ДОМА́ШНЕЕ ЗАДА́НИЕ / HOMEWORK

Three things, twenty minutes

1 — The introduction, five times, out loud

Chain all four together with no pause between them: name, country, city, job. Record it on your phone. The pauses on that recording are your actual curriculum — whatever you hesitate on is what lesson two gets built around.

2 — Decode twenty words at speed

Open the trainer's Декодер module and run one set. Report the average. Under 1.5 seconds per word means we skip decoding drills entirely and go straight to cases.

3 — Ten sentences off one frame

Take Я рабо́таю… and produce ten true sentences about your own life. Not translations of English sentences — sentences you build directly in the frame. If you can only make four, that number is the honest measure of where you are.
